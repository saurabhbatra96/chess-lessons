The future world champion Max Euwe was twenty when he played this game at The Hague in 1921. He follows your main line through 5.Nf3, then converts the small advantages it brings into a win by combining a bishop pin with a timely rook exchange.

Take White from move five.

```game
note: 5.Nf3 | Your main line. The knight develops and supports the pawn on e5.
note: 14.d4 | White fixes the centre, which opens the diagonal for the queen's bishop.
note: 18.Qxe6+ | With the queens off, the pawn on e6 becomes a lasting target.
note: 22.Re5 | The rook settles on an active square in front of the weak pawn.
note: 26.Bh3 | The bishop pins the e6-pawn to the king, and the target can no longer be defended.
note: 28.Bxe6+ | The bishop forks the king and the rook on c8.
```

Notice that the attack never needed a sacrifice. Euwe created one weak pawn and turned all of his pieces against it.
