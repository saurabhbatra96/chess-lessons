A fork is one piece attacking two things at once. Your opponent has one move, and one move rarely rescues both. It is the most common tactic at club level, and the knight is its natural instrument, since it attacks in a pattern that nothing else on the board can block.

The most valuable form is the fork with check, because the reply is forced and the second target simply falls.

```board
fen: r3k3/pp3ppp/8/3N4/8/8/PP3PPP/4K3 w - - 0 1
caption: White to play. The knight reaches a square that hits the king and the rook together.
best: d5c7
arrows: d5c7 c7e8 c7a8
```

## Train the Eye on the Targets

Do not hunt for forks by looking at your knight. Look at your opponent's king, queen and undefended rooks, and ask which squares attack two of them. Then ask whether any of your pieces can reach such a square. Working backwards from the targets is far quicker than trying each knight hop in turn.

Often the fork square is guarded, and the winning idea is to remove or lure away that guard first. Those techniques have lessons of their own later in this section.

## Pawns Fork Too

Any piece can fork, and the humblest fork is often the most profitable, because a pawn is happy to trade itself for anything.

```board
fen: r1bqkb1r/pppp1ppp/2n5/4p3/2B1N3/5N2/PPPP1PPP/R1BQK2R b KQkq - 0 5
caption: Black to play, having given up a knight on e4. One pawn move attacks bishop and knight, and the piece comes back.
best: d7d5
arrows: d7d5 d5c4 d5e4
```

This is the well-known fork trick from the Italian and Four Knights complex. Black sacrifices nothing in the end: the pawn fork regains the piece and leaves Black with a fine share of the centre.
