Attraction is the art of forcing an enemy piece onto a square where it will be harmed. Where deflection pulls a piece away from its duties, attraction draws it towards its doom. The usual bait is a sacrifice that simply cannot be declined, and the usual victim is the king.

```board
fen: 6k1/6pp/1q6/4N3/3P4/8/6PP/5RK1 w - - 0 1
caption: White to play. A knight check on d7 would hit the queen, if only the king stood on f8. So put it there.
best: f1f8
arrows: f1f8 e5d7
```

White plays Rf8+!, and the capture is forced, because every other square is covered or occupied. After Kxf8 the knight arrives on d7 with check and forks king and queen. A rook has been traded for a queen, and the only thing that made it possible was the king's obligation to take.

Notice how this combines with the previous lessons. Many forks, pins and skewers are one square away from working. Attraction is how you supply that missing square.

## Offers You Can Afford to Refuse

Not every sacrifice is a forced one, and an opponent who takes unwisely may lose at once. The most famous example in the opening repertoire is the mate that bears the name of Kermeur de Legal.

```board
fen: rn1qkbnr/ppp2p1p/3p2p1/4p3/2B1P1b1/2N2N2/PPPP1PPP/R1BQK2R w KQkq - 0 5
caption: White to play. The knight seems pinned to the queen. It moves anyway.
best: f3e5
arrows: f3e5
```

If Black greedily plays ...Bxd1, then Bxf7+ Ke7 Nd5 is mate, with the three minor pieces doing everything. The correct response is to take the knight with the pawn instead, after which White plays Qxg4 and is simply a pawn up. Either way, the pin was an illusion.

## How to Look for It

Ask of each target: *which square would I like this piece to stand on?* Then ask whether a check or capture can force it there. Sacrifices stop seeming magical once you begin by imagining the finished position.
