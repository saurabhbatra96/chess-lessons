A bishop only ever moves on squares of one colour. If your own central pawns are fixed on that colour, the bishop bumps into them wherever it goes. That is what we mean by a bad bishop: not a badly placed bishop, but one that is obstructed by its own pawns.

```board
after: 1.e4 e6 2.d4 d5 3.e5 c5 4.c3 Nc6 5.Nf3
caption: Black's bishop on c8 has pawns on d5 and e6 in front of it. As a French player, you will get to know this piece very well.
```

## What to Do About a Bad Bishop

You have three options, and each is worth checking.

- **Exchange it**, especially for your opponent's good bishop.
- **Get it outside the pawn chain** before the pawns close, which is why the bishop often moves early in the Exchange French.
- **Move the pawns.** A pawn break can change a bad bishop into a good one.

In French and Queen's Gambit structures, the most common solution is ...b6 and ...Ba6, offering to exchange it for White's good light-squared bishop.

```choice
fen: r1b2rk1/4qppp/1pn1p3/p2p4/3P4/2PBPN2/P4PPP/R2Q1RK1 b - - 0 12
question: Black's light-squared bishop is obstructed by the pawns on d5 and e6. What is the most useful thing to do with it?
answer: c8a6
option: c8a6 | The bishop offers itself in exchange for White's most dangerous piece. The rook on a8 protects it, thanks to the a-pawn having moved on.
option: c8b7 | The bishop develops, but it still looks straight at its own pawn on d5 and remains a bad bishop.
option: a8a7 | A slow rook move that leaves the problem piece on c8, where it blocks the rest of Black's development.
```

## The Pair of Bishops

Two bishops together cover every square on the board, and in open positions they are a real asset. If you have the pair, try to open the game. If your opponent has them, try to keep it closed.
