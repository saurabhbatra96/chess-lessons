A pawn chain is a diagonal line of pawns, each defending the next. It controls space but has an inherent weakness: the pawn at the base has no pawn protecting it. That pawn is where the chain can be attacked, and Nimzowitsch's advice, which is still good, was to attack the chain at its base.

In the French, White's chain runs from b2 or c3 up to e5. Black has two levers: ...c5 hits the base at d4, and ...f6 hits the head at e5.

```choice
after: 1.e4 e6 2.d4 d5 3.e5
question: White has built the chain. What should Black do at once?
answer: c7c5
option: c7c5 | The strike at the base. Black opens the c-file and begins the pressure on d4 that drives the whole French.
option: h7h6 | A slow move that gives White time to reinforce the centre with c3 and Nf3.
option: g7g5 | A random flank advance that weakens Black's own kingside and does nothing about the centre.
```

## The Second Lever

When the first lever has done its work, ...f6 is the second. It attacks the head of the chain and opens the f-file for Black's rook, often after castling kingside. You can see Botvinnik use both levers in the model game in the Classics section.

## The Other Side of the Chain

White has a plan as well. White's chain points at the kingside, and space there can be used to attack the king. So Black must time the levers well. When you are unsure which to play, look at where your pieces already stand, then choose the lever they support.
