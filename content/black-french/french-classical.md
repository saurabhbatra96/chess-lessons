After 3.Nc3 you have several options. This repertoire uses the Classical 3...Nf6, which develops a piece, attacks e4, and leads to the kind of pawn chain you already know how to fight.

## The Steinitz: 4.e5

White drives the knight away and supports the chain with f4. Black strikes at d4 with the c-pawn, as always.

```line
side: b
caption: The Steinitz Variation
moves: 1.e4 e6 2.d4 d5 3.Nc3 Nf6 4.e5 Nfd7 5.f4 c5 6.Nf3 Nc6 7.Be3 cxd4 8.Nxd4 Bc5 9.Qd2 Bxd4 10.Bxd4 Nxd4 11.Qxd4 Qb6
note: 4...Nfd7 | The knight heads for b6 or supports ...c5 and ...f6.
note: 5...c5 | The usual blow at the base of the chain.
note: 8...Bc5 | The bishop pins down the knight on d4.
note: 11...Qb6 | Offering a queen trade, which removes White's attacking chances.
```

## 4.Bg5: The Pin

White pins the knight and prepares e5. Black breaks the pin with the bishop and is content to see the dark-squared bishops exchanged.

```line
side: b
caption: 4.Bg5 Be7
moves: 1.e4 e6 2.d4 d5 3.Nc3 Nf6 4.Bg5 Be7 5.e5 Nfd7 6.Bxe7 Qxe7 7.f4 a6 8.Nf3 c5 9.dxc5 Nc6
note: 4...Be7 | A simple way of unpinning.
note: 7...a6 | Preventing Nb5, a square White's pieces would like to use.
note: 8...c5 | Again, the strike at the chain.
note: 9...Nc6 | Black develops first, and the pawn on c5 can be recovered soon.
```

Once you have followed these lines through, compare the patterns. In every variation of the French, Black aims a pawn at the white chain, and that is the plan to fall back on whenever you cannot remember the theory.
